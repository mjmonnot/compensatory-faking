# Online Supplement

# Supplemental Materials for: Compensatory Faking and the Relationship Between Cognitive Ability and Personality in Personnel Selection

This supplement contains the benchmark comparison against published database estimates (Table S1), the full five-context meta-analytic estimates (Table S2), robustness and bias analyses (Tables S3–S4), era and within-applicant moderator analyses (Table S5), the analytic composite grid (Table S6, Figure S1), Monte Carlo sensitivity runs with fitted inflation parameters (Table S7), a sensitivity analysis appending the operational mega-samples reported by Schilling et al. (2021) (Table S8), prevalence sensitivity runs for both Monte Carlo models (Table S9), the six-target refit of the Study 2 inflation functional forms (Table S10), selection outcomes for every trait–faking anchor set under a uniform reporting rule (Table S11), sample-level forest plots for the four Big Five traits not shown in the main text (Figures S2–S5), and a forest plot of Conscientiousness applicant samples split by region (Figure S6). All values are produced by the scripted pipeline described in the manuscript's Method section.

*Note.* Column headers give the assumed faking prevalence. The 30%–50% range comes from within-person applicant studies (Donovan et al., 2014; Griffith et al., 2007); 20% is the lower bound of the Griffith and Converse (2011) evidence synthesis, which concludes 30% ±10%; 40% is the primary specification reported in the main text. Prevalence denotes the proportion of applicants who fake at all (the any-scale rate the within-person studies report), matching each model's binary faker indicator. All model parameters were refit at each prevalence so that every pool reproduces the same empirical targets. Study 1 = random-faker Monte Carlo model (main-text Table 4); Study 2 = trait-driven model, field-conservative anchors with targeted linear inflation (main-text Table 6). % changed = percentage of top-down hires that differ from the honest counterfactual; Loss % = percentage reduction in mean true performance of hires; SR = selection ratio. Because the fitted inflation parameters must reproduce the same pool-level evidence at any prevalence, lower prevalence concentrates larger inflation in fewer applicants and produces worse outcomes; the one exception (Study 2, non-US, SR = .10, 40% vs. 50%) is a 0.4-point difference within Monte Carlo error. The Study 1 cells at 20% prevalence should be read as an upper bound on harm rather than as a realistic scenario: reproducing the Birkeland et al. (2006) mean shift with so few fakers requires per-faker mean inflation of 3.0–4.8 *SD*, which bounded response scales would not permit; the trait-driven Study 2 model, which fits inflation against six targets including the observed variance ratio, degrades far more gradually.

*Note.* All three functional forms were fit against the same six empirical targets described in the Method (main text): Linear = the primary form (main-text Tables 5–6); Censored = inflation floored at zero on all inflated scales; Freed skill = separate skill slopes for the Conscientiousness/Emotional Stability scales versus the self-esteem scale.
